### Stephen Van Evera — *Guide to Methods for Students of Political Science*

**Assigned:** pp. 7–48, 115–116, 123–128

**Quick hook:**  
Good theory makes causal claims explicit, testable, falsifiable, and useful for comparing competing explanations.

### Paul C. Avey et al. — “Does Social Science Inform Foreign Policy? Evidence from a Survey of US National Security, Trade, and Development Officials”

**Citation:**  
*International Studies Quarterly*, Vol. 66, No. 1 (2022), pp. 1–19.

**Quick hook:**  
Policymakers use social science more than conventional wisdom suggests, but national-security officials use it less directly than officials working in trade and development.

### Stacie E. Goddard and Joshua D. Kertzer — “How to Put IR Theory Into Practice”

**Citation:**  
*Foreign Affairs*, October 31, 2025.

**Quick hook:**  
Policymakers cannot escape theory. Their underlying paradigms shape which facts they notice, how they diagnose problems, and which policies appear sensible.

**Links:**

- Foreign Affairs:  
  https://www.foreignaffairs.com/united-states/how-put-ir-theory-practice

### Jack S. Levy — “The Causes of War and the Conditions of Peace”

**Citation:**  
*Annual Review of Political Science*, Vol. 1 (1998), pp. 139–165.

**Quick hook:**  
There is no single master cause of war. Progress comes from identifying causal mechanisms, combining levels of analysis, improving research design, and using multiple methodologies.

**Links:**

# Lesson 2 — Realism and the Role of Power

## Kenneth N. Waltz — “The Origins of War in Neorealist Theory”

**Citation:**  
*Journal of Interdisciplinary History*, Vol. 18, No. 4 (Spring 1988).

**Quick hook:**  
States do not have to be inherently aggressive for conflict to occur. Anarchy, uncertainty, and self-help generate recurring competition among states seeking security.

**Links:**

- Public PDF:  
  https://pestuge.iliauni.edu.ge/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/Kenneth-N.-Waltz-The-Origins-of-War-in-Neorealist-Theory.pdf

## Robert Jervis — “Cooperation Under the Security Dilemma”

**Assigned:**  
Reprinted in *Essential Readings in World Politics*, 3rd ed., pp. 346–359.

**Quick hook:**  
The security dilemma is conditional. Cooperation becomes easier when defense has the advantage and states can distinguish defensive military preparations from offensive ones.

## Helen Milner — “The Assumption of Anarchy in International Relations Theory: A Critique”

**Citation:**  
*Review of International Studies*, Vol. 17, No. 1 (January 1991), pp. 67–85.

**Quick hook:**  
Anarchy exists, but neorealists place too much explanatory weight on it. International politics is also structured by interdependence, bargaining, institutions, and relationships among states.

**Links:**

## John J. Mearsheimer — *The Tragedy of Great Power Politics*

**Assigned:**  
pp. 1–54.

**Quick hook:**  
Because great powers cannot know one another’s future intentions, survival pushes them to maximize relative power. The safest possible position is regional hegemony.

**Links:**

## Jessica Chen Weiss — “Don’t Panic About Taiwan: Alarm Over a Chinese Invasion Could Become a Self-Fulfilling Prophecy”

**Citation:**

**Quick hook:**  
Treating war as imminent can itself worsen the security dilemma. Effective deterrence should be paired with reassurance so that defensive measures do not create the escalation they are intended to prevent.

# Lesson 3 — Liberalism and the Role of Institutions

## Robert O. Keohane — *After Hegemony: Cooperation and Discord in the World Political Economy*

**Assigned:**  
pp. 65–84 — Chapter 5, “Rational-Choice and Functional Explanations”

**Quick hook:**  
Even in an anarchic international system, states can cooperate when institutions reduce uncertainty, provide information, lower transaction costs, and make reciprocal behavior easier.

**Citation:**  
*International Security*, Vol. 23, No. 3 (Winter 1998–1999), pp. 43–78.

## Sebastian Rosato — “The Flawed Logic of Democratic Peace Theory”

**Citation:**  
*American Political Science Review*, Vol. 97, No. 4 (November 2003), pp. 585–602.

**Quick hook:**  
Peace among democracies may be real, but Rosato argues that the standard causal mechanisms offered by democratic peace theory—norms, accountability, institutional constraints, and transparency—do not adequately explain it.

## Joseph S. Nye, Jr. — “The Information Revolution and Soft Power”

**Citation:**  
*Current History*, Vol. 113 (January 2014), pp. 19–22.

**Quick hook:**  
Power is not limited to coercion or payment. The information revolution lowers barriers to participation and increases the importance of attraction, credibility, networks, and persuasion—what Nye calls soft power.

Lesson 3 Discussion Questions

* What are the key characteristics of war as Clausewitz understands it? Is it a rational, analytical process? If not, how does Clausewitz suggest it be managed?
* Think about the Clausewitzian trinity as Villacres and Bassford describe it. Apply it to a war of your choice. Does the trinity produce a deeper understanding of the conflict?
* What are the timeless aspects of war versus the shifting characteristics of warfare?
* How does Clausewitz differentiate between the inherent nature and character of war, and why is the distinction important?
* What are Clausewitz’s concepts of fog and friction, and how do they affect strategy and military decision-making?
* How are Clausewitz and Jomini different? How does each understand the role of scientific analysis in war?
